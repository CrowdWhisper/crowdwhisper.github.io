**The idea.** A blockchain is a shared record of payments and other events, kept by many computers at once. Each new page in that record — a "block" — is locked to the page before it. Change an old entry and the lock breaks, which the rest of the network can see. The aim is to let people transfer value without handing a single company the master ledger. Bitcoin put a public version of this to work in 2009 <a class="cite" href="#src-1">[1]</a><a class="cite" href="#src-2">[2]</a>. Later networks kept the shared-record idea and changed the recipe: Ethereum added programs that run on the chain, the XRP Ledger was built for payments, and dollar tokens such as USDC now move on several of these networks <a class="cite" href="#src-3">[3]</a><a class="cite" href="#src-4">[4]</a><a class="cite" href="#src-5">[5]</a><a class="cite" href="#src-6">[6]</a>.

{% capture t %}**Blocks, fingerprints and agreement.** A block is a bundle of recent transactions plus a short fingerprint — a "hash" — of the previous block [1]. A hash is a number computed from the data; change even one character and you get a different hash. Because each block carries the last block's hash, the pages form a chain. The computers that keep a copy and enforce the rules are called nodes.

They still have to agree which block comes next. That agreement is called consensus. Bitcoin's method is proof of work: "miners" compete to solve a puzzle that takes real electricity, and the winner proposes the next block, on average about every ten minutes [1][7][8]. Rewriting an old payment would mean redoing all that work for every block that came after it [1]. Ethereum used a similar puzzle until 15 September 2022, then switched to proof of stake: "validators" lock up ether as a security deposit, and the protocol picks one of them to propose a block in each twelve-second slot [9][10]. Dishonest behaviour can cost them some or all of that deposit [10]. Proof of stake is not a free lunch — it trades electricity for capital at risk — but Ethereum's own site, citing the Crypto Carbon Ratings Institute, says The Merge cut the network's electricity use by more than 99.988%, to about 0.0026 terawatt-hours a year, and cut its estimated carbon emissions from 11,016,000 tonnes of CO2e to 870 [11].{% endcapture %}

{% capture t %}**A short history.** The chain-of-hashes idea is older than Bitcoin. In 1991, Stuart Haber and W. Scott Stornetta published a method for time-stamping a digital document so that it could not be quietly back-dated, even with help from the time-stamping service [12]. Bitcoin's design paper cites that work [1]. On 31 October 2008, a person or group using the name Satoshi Nakamoto sent the paper to a cryptography mailing list [13]. Bitcoin's first block, dated 3 January 2009, embeds a headline from that day's *Times* of London: "Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks" [2]. Issuance is written to stop at 21 million coins [7][8].

The XRP Ledger, designed for payments rather than mining, first launched in June 2012; its documents say a new ledger is agreed in about three to six seconds [4][14]. On 30 July 2015, Ethereum went live as "Frontier", adding smart contracts: programs that the chain itself runs [3][15]. In 2026 Solana, a later proof-of-stake network, adopted a new agreement method called Alpenglow. Its authors said they expected finality of about 150 milliseconds in simulations — a design target, not a figure we measured ourselves [16][17].{% endcapture %}

{% capture t %}**What it gives you, and what it costs.** Public blockchains are transparent: anyone can look up a payment. They are durable: once many blocks have been stacked on top, changing the past is deliberately expensive [1]. They are also decentralised, in the sense that no single office has a master "undo" button — though how widely control is actually spread differs from one network to the next, and a coordinated change of the rules is still possible. Ethereum itself agreed a software change in 2016 after a large theft from a contract [18]. "Immutable" is a practical claim, not a law of nature.

The trade-offs are the point. Bitcoin's slow pace and electricity use are part of how it resists rewriting. Faster chains confirm in seconds, with different assumptions about who must be trusted. A base layer that everyone used for tiny payments would clog. That is why Ethereum has "layer 2" networks, or rollups, that bundle many transactions and settle them back on Ethereum [19].{% endcapture %}

{% capture t %}**Layers, programs and data from the outside.** "Layer 1" is the base chain — Bitcoin, Ethereum, Solana. "Layer 2" sits on top. Ethereum's site describes rollups as networks that store their data on Ethereum itself, so they inherit its security while aiming for cheaper transactions [19]. Base, built with the OP Stack, is one example listed there [19].

Smart contracts are programs stored on a chain, most famously on Ethereum. They run exactly as written, including the mistakes [15]. A contract cannot by itself see a stock price or a weather report. An "oracle" is a service that feeds that outside data in. Ethereum's documentation says data from an oracle must be correct for a smart contract to execute correctly, and that a corrupt oracle means contracts will run on bad data [20]. One working example: in November 2026, Pyth Network began distributing the New York Stock Exchange's real-time quotes through its marketplace, with clients still licensing the data from NYSE [21][22].{% endcapture %}

{% capture t %}**What people actually use them for.** Bitcoin is used as digital cash and as a long-term holding, with a known cap of 21 million coins [7][8]. Ether is the fuel and the stake for a chain that runs applications: lending markets, exchanges and digital collectibles [3][15]. Solana is used in a similar way, with an emphasis on speed [16][17]. The XRP Ledger was built for payments, and settles in a few seconds without mining [4][14]. USDC is a dollar stablecoin issued by Circle. Circle says it is fully backed and redeemable one-for-one for US dollars, with reserves reported weekly and checked in attestation reports [5][6]. Tokens like USDC live on several blockchains at once; they are not a blockchain themselves.

None of this is a reason to buy or sell anything. A coin's design does not tell you its price tomorrow.{% endcapture %}

{% capture t %}**Today and tomorrow.** These networks are already used for payments, applications and dollar tokens. Officials are writing rules around them: in October 2026 the European Banking Authority named USDC the first "significant" e-money token in the EU [23][24]. What comes next is not a forecast. Teams are building cheaper second layers, and more traditional assets are being tried on-chain. The trade-offs — speed, energy, and how decentralised a network really is — will still be there. A shared record that strangers can check, without a central clerk, is a powerful idea. It is not magic, and it is not financial advice.{% endcapture %}
