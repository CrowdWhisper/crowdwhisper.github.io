**The idea.** Mining is how some cryptocurrencies are issued and secured: machines try hashes until one finds a valid proof of work, and that miner takes the block reward. That now covers a laptop program, a palm-sized lucky miner, a loud warehouse machine, and a heater that throws off a little bitcoin as a by-product. The starting sum is always expected coins times price, minus electricity. We use figures from 12 November 2026, and we show the sums so you can redo them.

Electricity is the swing factor, and real tariffs vary by place and even by hour. We use a **fictional example** of $0.10 per kWh, and on the charts a cheaper illustrative point at $0.05 and a dearer one at $0.20.

{% capture t %}**Scripts on your own PC.** Bitcoin on a home computer is pointless. Its algorithm, SHA-256, has been dominated for more than a decade by purpose-built ASIC chips: a credit-card-sized lucky miner already does about 1 terahash per second (TH/s), and a desktop is orders of magnitude slower. The Bitcoin network was running at about 931 exahashes per second over the three days to 12 November, against a difficulty of 148.36 trillion [1].

Monero is different. Its proof of work, RandomX, is designed for ordinary CPUs, so specialised chips have little edge [2][3]. People often run the open-source program XMRig [4]. As an illustration — your chip will differ — take 12,000 hashes per second at 120 watts. On 12 November the Monero network was about 7.06 billion hashes per second, the last block paid 0.626 XMR including fees, and Monero was about $551 [5][6]. That is about $0.42 a day after a 1% pool fee. At the fictional $0.10 per kWh, power costs $0.29, for an expected $0.13 a day; at the illustrative $0.20 rate it is a loss.

- **Pros:** little extra hardware if you already have a computer; you can stop at any time.
- **Cons:** tiny expected income, erased if Monero's price falls; heat, noise and wear.
- **A warning:** never run a miner from a random website. Cryptojacking — malware or a webpage that mines without asking — is a known risk. The US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency warns that it can wreck performance and raise the power bill [7].{% endcapture %}

{% capture t %}**Lucky miners and solo boxes.** Open-source Bitaxe devices, and similar commercial lucky-miner boxes, put a single modern ASIC chip on a board the size of a credit card. CoinDesk, describing the Bitaxe Gamma, puts it at about 1 to 1.3 TH/s and 15 to 21 watts, costing about $60 to $150 [8]. The open-source design uses the BM1370 chip also found in the Antminer S21 Pro [9].

At 1 TH/s and today's difficulty, the expected time to find a bitcoin block alone is about 20,200 years. The chance of at least one block in a given year is about 0.005%, or 1 in 20,200 [1]. That is an expected value, not a forecast; a win this year is still possible. Expected revenue is about 3 cents a day; at the fictional $0.10 per kWh and 20 watts, power costs about 5 cents, a small expected loss, with a rare chance of the full reward — about 3.15 bitcoin, or about $225,000 at the 12 November price [5][1].

Documented cases, with the pool and reward checked on the chain: on 10 March 2025, block 887,212 paid 3.150 bitcoin through Solo CKPool; Cointelegraph reported the pool's operator saying it came from a 480-gigahash Bitaxe [10][1]. On 10 July 2026 at 03:30 UTC, block 957,382 paid 3.138 bitcoin through Public Pool; CoinDesk reported a Bitaxe at 995 gigahashes per second [8][1].

- **Pros:** cheap, quiet and small; if you hit, you take the block reward (a solo service such as Solo CKPool charges a 2% fee [11]).
- **Cons:** expected income is a few cents a day; you can run for a lifetime and never hit.{% endcapture %}

{% capture t %}**Heaters, cloud mining, staking and merged mining.** Some makers sell room heaters with mining chips inside. One lists models from 40 TH/s at 1.0 kW to 120 TH/s at 2.2 kW, at 35 to 40 decibels [14]. Using its 60 TH/s, 1.1 kW figures: expected revenue about $1.81 a day after a 1% pool fee; at the fictional $0.10 per kWh, power costs $2.64, a $0.83 loss — unless you would have used a resistive heater anyway, in which case the bitcoin is a rebate on heat you already needed. Another maker lists a home mining heater at $1,249 [15].

Cloud mining — paying a stranger to mine for you — has a long record of fraud. In 2021 the US SEC charged a man with offering a purported cloud-mining programme whose promises, the SEC says, were untrue, and with taking nearly all of the money raised [16]. The UK's FCA has warned the public off an unauthorised firm marketing "Cloud Mining City" [17]. If you cannot verify the machines, and especially if daily returns are promised, walk away.

Staking is not mining. You lock coins to help secure a proof-of-stake network. Ethereum's site showed a current annual rate of about 2.5% on 12 November [18]. There is no ASIC to buy, and the risks are different: the coin's price, exit queues, and slashing if a validator misbehaves [18]. Merged mining lets a Bitcoin miner offer the same work to a second chain, such as Namecoin, for a little extra revenue at no extra electricity [19].{% endcapture %}
